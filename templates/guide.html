1. What do we mean by another “dimension”?

We use dimension in two different senses. Space has three independent directions. A state can require many independent numbers to describe it. A pendulum, for example, needs both an angle and an angular velocity to predict its motion. Its state space is larger than a picture of its position alone.

Quantum states have their own mathematical spaces. A normalized pure two-qubit state has six independent real parameters once the physically irrelevant overall phase is removed. A general mixed two-qubit state has fifteen. Neither count adds directions to the room.

Our proposal: keep three spatial dimensions, choose a way to label time slices, and attach the relevant geometry, fields and quantum-state information to each slice.

2. A compact record of one slice

\[\mathcal U_t=(\Sigma_t,h_{ij},K_{ij},\rho_t),\qquad \mathcal T=\{\mathcal U_t:t\in I\}.\]

Σt is the spatial slice. The spatial metric hij gives its internal distances. Kij is its extrinsic curvature: how it sits in spacetime. The density operator ρt describes the quantum degrees of freedom retained in a specified approximation. Together, these records make the tunnel history 𝒯.

This is a schematic classical-geometry/quantum-matter record, not a general quantum state of spacetime. The matter Hilbert space and quantum evolution need a specified model. Dynamical gravitational data must obey constraints; arbitrary h, K and matter cannot simply be assembled into a solution. A global stack of spatial slices also requires suitable spacetime conditions, such as global hyperbolicity.

For classical fields, replace ρ with the required field values and conjugate momenta, subject to their constraints. For quantum matter on a prescribed background, geometry is supplied externally. A self-consistent semiclassical calculation needs a renormalized stress-energy expectation value and its own domain checks. Our three demos do not solve that combined problem.

The Standard Model uses quantum field theory with gauge structure

\[SU(3)_c\times SU(2)_L\times U(1)_Y.\]

The first factor describes the strong interaction. The other two form the electroweak structure; electromagnetic U(1) emerges as the unbroken subgroup after electroweak symmetry breaking. Quantum mechanics is part of this framework. Gravity is what GR describes. Counting these labels as separate, interchangeable axes would count some ingredients twice.

4. Dynamics makes the tunnel useful

In the quantum lab, an excitation can be shared between two sites. Their effective exchange Hamiltonian is

\[H=\frac{\hbar J}{2}(X_A X_B+Y_A Y_B).\]

X and Y here are Pauli operators, not spatial coordinates. Starting with A excited and with no noise, the state evolves as

\[|\psi(\tau)\rangle=\cos(J\tau)|10\rangle-i\sin(J\tau)|01\rangle.\]

At Jτ = π/4 the local excitation probabilities are both 1/2 and the pair is maximally entangled. A classical mixture of |10⟩ and |01⟩ has the same local probabilities but no entanglement. That is why the wall markings alone cannot tell the whole story.

\[\rho_{\mathrm{mix}}=\tfrac12|10\rangle\langle10|+\tfrac12|01\rangle\langle01|.\]

The app adds a specified Lindblad dephasing channel and computes a density matrix analytically. MODEL.md gives the generator, reduced equations and clock assumptions. The links inside the drawing encode concurrence, not a physical route through empty space.

5. Let geometry evolve, too

The expansion experiment models a homogeneous and isotropic universe. Its scale factor a tells us how distances between comoving points change. The Friedmann equation relates its expansion rate to matter, radiation, curvature and a cosmological constant:

\[\frac{H^2}{H_{\mathrm{ref}}^2}=\frac{\Omega_r}{a^4}+\frac{\Omega_m}{a^3}+\frac{\Omega_k}{a^2}+\Omega_\Lambda.\]

The solver evolves acceleration as well as position, allowing expansion to turn into contraction. A freely travelling test photon follows

\[\lambda/\lambda_{\mathrm{emit}}=a/a_{\mathrm{emit}},\qquad E/E_{\mathrm{emit}}=a_{\mathrm{emit}}/a.\]

These connect geometry, light and photon energy. The reference scales and ideal-fluid assumptions are documented in COSMOLOGY.md. The small bound quantum lab is a separate controlled experiment; its coupling has not been arbitrarily rescaled with cosmic expansion.

6. A place for the Standard Model and gravity

An established way to organize matter and gravitational physics at low energies is an effective field theory. In units ℏ = c = 1, its local action can be sketched as

\[S_{\mathrm{eff}}=\int d^4x\sqrt{-g}\,\bigg[\frac{M_{\mathrm{Pl}}^2}{2}(R-2\Lambda)+\mathcal L_{\mathrm{SM}}+\alpha R^2+\beta_R R_{\mu\nu}R^{\mu\nu}+\cdots\bigg].\]

MPl is the reduced Planck mass, R measures spacetime curvature, Λ is the cosmological constant and ℒSM contains Standard Model fields and interactions. The dots include further symmetry-allowed curvature and matter operators with appropriate coefficients. This is an organizing framework; it does not supply a complete high-energy quantum-gravity theory. None of the interactive demos numerically solves this action.

It provides a disciplined way to grow our thought experiment: specify a tractable sector, state its approximation, connect it to the existing variables, and test a limit with a known answer.

7. Does time itself flow?

The tunnel pictures an ordered history. Its time parameter does not require a second external time in which the whole universe moves down the pipe. A wave drawn along the tunnel can represent a changing field, but it does not demonstrate that time is a physical wave.

The arrow of time is another question. The dephasing model describes a subsystem losing accessible coherence to an environment. It does not derive the universe’s low-entropy initial conditions or settle why we experience a present moment.
